Five prototypes of one purchase

Nothing in this area can be bought. These are five prototypes of a purchase flow for work that is fulfilled by people — mapping what the agents a company already runs were granted, what they are expected to do, and writing the policies that would close the gap. Five interfaces, one model, and one JSON brief that every one of them produces identically. The interface is the variable. The document is not.

What is actually being sold here

Two things, and neither of them is a scan.

A map. Every agent surface a company runs, the credential behind it, and what that credential permits. Then the other half: what each one is actually authorised and expected to do. The gap between the two lists is the product — permitted, and nothing anybody wrote down would need it.

A policy set that would close it. Written against the estate that was just described, one policy per gap, each naming the grant it constrains. Not a template with a company name substituted in.

A named security professional does this work. Nothing in it runs by itself today, the seven-role team these prototypes describe is a specification rather than something staffed, and which parts of it could later be done without people is a decision nobody has taken. delivered by a person 11 Sep 2026 So what these pages produce is the brief that team starts from — the thing a first call would otherwise be spent assembling.

The same question, from three sides

The three buyers on this site arrive at the same map from different directions, and the configurator changes its frame accordingly. Who each one is for.

Arrives askingWhat the map is drawn for
You are a startupWhat does our estate look like to somebody who is about to ask?Map it upwards. Diligence is coming, the questions are the same every time, and the difference is whether the answers already exist in writing
You are an investorWhat is the agent in this company actually doing, and what was it allowed to do?Map both sides of a company you do not operate: the permitted half from its credentials, the expected half from its own account

The model all five render

Every prototype reads one file. The sections below are the questions, the weight column is what moves the price, and both are published here because a page that asks somebody twenty questions owes them the list before they start, and a weighting a reader cannot see is a price a reader cannot check.

SectionOptionsWeightWhy it is asked
1. What is running
Which of these is already in your environment?		10×1Every one you name becomes a node in the map. Anything you do not name cannot be mapped, and the brief says which ones you were unsure about rather than leaving them out.
2. What it was granted
What does the credential behind each of those actually permit?		12×1This is the permitted half of the delta. Tick what the credential CAN do, not what anybody intends to use it for — that is the next question, and keeping them apart is the entire method.
3. What it is supposed to do
What is each of them actually authorised and expected to do?		9×1This is the expected half. The delta is the gap between this list and the one above, so a brief with an empty list here has nothing to compute and says so on its own face.
4. What you want out of it
Which of these should the team produce?		9×1Each line is a deliverable with a state. Two of them do not exist yet, they are on the list anyway, and the brief carries the reason rather than dropping the row.
5. How the answers are evidenced
How much of this should be taken on your word?		3pick oneThe cheapest version of this work is your own account, written down properly. The expensive version is somebody checking it. Both are real products and the difference is stated rather than assumed.

The configurator does not compute a price. Weighted selections add up, the total lands in a band, and the band names one of the four tiers that already exist and its price. Nothing here invents a number — a price is the first thing the pack says may not be invented, and a checkout that emitted "£347" would be inventing one with extra steps. A build check fails the release if a band names an offer that is not on the offer list. exists and runs 11 Sep 2026

The prebaked starts are hypotheses. Nobody has counted how common any of those five shapes is. Each brief records which one it started from, so the team reading it can tell what was assumed from what was answered — which is the only thing that makes starting from a guess safe rather than fast. specified, not built 11 Sep 2026

What a brief carries, including what it does not know

The output is one JSON document. Beside the estate, the grants, the mandates and the deliverables, every brief carries two fields that most configurators would not emit:

  • excess_authority — the grants you ticked that no mandate on the brief would need. Computed from edges published in the model, so a reader can disagree with a specific edge rather than with a verdict.
  • unknowns — what this brief could not establish. An empty expected column. A deliverable whose thing does not exist yet. A grant that a named surface normally carries and you removed. A brief that names its own gaps is worth more to the team reading it than one that reads as complete and is not, and this is the same discipline the ledger applies to the rest of the site.

What happens to it

Nothing, unless you send it. A brief is written to your browser's own local storage and reaches nothing else. There is no form, input, textarea or select anywhere in this site's output, and no page that sells anything opens a network connection — both are build checks rather than intentions — so exporting a brief hands the file to you. exists and runs 11 Sep 2026
